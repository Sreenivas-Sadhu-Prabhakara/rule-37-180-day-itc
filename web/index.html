How the clock is worked out

      
Under the second proviso to section 16(2) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, credit taken on a purchase is conditional on paying the supplier the value of the supply along with the tax on it within 180 days of the date the supplier issued the invoice. Miss that, and rule 37 of the CGST Rules, 2017 requires the credit to be reversed — in proportion to whatever is left unpaid.

      
So for each invoice this tool computes:

      
    
        
  • Day 180 — the invoice date plus 180 days, which is the last day the supplier can be paid.
    • 
        
  • What was unpaid on that day, from the payments recorded against the invoice.
    • 
        
  • The reversal — the credit availed, times the unpaid amount, divided by the invoice total, head by head.
    • 
        
  • The return it goes in — the GSTR-3B for the tax period immediately following the period day 180 falls in, and the day that return is due.
    • 
        
  • The re-claim — rule 37(2) lets you take the credit again once the supplier is paid, in the return for the period you pay in. Rule 37(4) keeps the section 16(4) time limit out of the way, so a payment years later still restores it.
    • 
      

      
A part payment reverses a part of the credit, which is what makes paying something worth doing. Pay ₹50,000 of a ₹1,18,000 invoice carrying ₹18,000 of credit and ₹10,372.88 reverses instead of ₹18,000.

      
Which supplier to pay first is not the biggest invoice. It is the one protecting the most credit per rupee, which is the one taxed highest: ₹1,00,000 paid against a 28 per cent purchase saves ₹21,875 of credit, the same money against a 5 per cent purchase saves ₹4,762. The payment plan ranks on exactly that, with the nearest deadline breaking a tie.


      
Where the reversal is reported

      
Per Circular No. 170/02/2022-GST dated 6 July 2022, a rule 37 reversal goes in table 4(B)(2) of FORM GSTR-3B — the reversals that are not permanent and can be reclaimed — and the later re-claim in table 4(A)(5), disclosed in table 4(D)(1). Reported anywhere else it becomes a permanent reversal, does not reach the portal's reversal ledger, and leaves no balance to re-claim against. The working paper above states the table against every figure.


      

        The figures used, and what you can change
        
    
          
  • 180 days, from the second proviso to s.16(2) and rule 37(1). Changeable, in case the period is amended.
    • 
          
  • Counted from the day after the invoice date, so day 180 is the invoice date plus 180 days. This is a convention rather than something the rule spells out — it follows s.9 of the General Clauses Act, 1897, under which "from" excludes the first day. It changes the answer only where day 180 falls at a month end, and the date is shown against every invoice so you can check it.
    • 
          
  • The reversal goes in the return for the period immediately following the one the 180 days expire in, which is what rule 37(1) says. It is not the period the 181st day falls in: those coincide only when day 180 is the last day of a period.
    • 
          
  • GSTR-3B due on the 20th for a monthly filer, the 22nd or 24th for a quarterly one depending on the State or Union territory of your principal place of business — rule 61(1). Chandigarh and Delhi are in the 24th group.
    • 
          
  • Interest at 18% a year, the rate notified under s.50(3). Charged here only on a reversal made after the return it belonged in fell due, and labelled indicative. Whether any interest arises on a timely reversal is unsettled: s.50(3) reaches credit "wrongly availed and utilised", and rule 88B(3) runs the clock from the date of utilisation. Set the rate to nil to take that view, or switch the basis to run from the return in which the credit was taken to see the larger exposure.
    • 
          
  • Out of scope: supplies on which you pay the tax yourself under reverse charge, Schedule I supplies made without consideration, and amounts added under s.15(2)(b) — the last two deemed paid by the provisos to rule 37(1). Mark them in the register and they are counted out, with the reason shown.
    • 
          
  • Ageing bands of 0–60, 61–120, 121–150, 151–180 and over 180 days, chosen around the deadline rather than the 30-day steps of an ordinary payables ageing.
    • 
          
  • All amounts are held in whole paise, so the totals are exact. The figures staged for the return are rounded to whole rupees, as s.170 requires, head by head — each head is its own column in the return.
    • 
          
  • An invoice total left blank is taken as the taxable value plus the credit heads. Where you took less credit than the supplier charged, enter the real invoice total: the proportion is computed against what you owe, not against the credit.
    • 
        

      


      
This tool works out figures from a register you give it. It is not tax advice and it files nothing. It cannot tell you whether a credit was eligible in the first place, and it has no way of knowing about debit notes, credit notes, imports or anything not in the file. Rule 37 was substituted with effect from 1 October 2022 and this implements that version. Check every figure against your own invoices and ledger, and confirm the law with the GST portal or a qualified practitioner, before you file.
